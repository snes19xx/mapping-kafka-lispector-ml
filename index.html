Mapping Kafka and Lispector

Kafka died in 1924, when Clarice Lispector was three. Critics have paired them ever since: the beetle and the cockroach, the locked door and the white wardrobe. This is a star map of 5,324 of their paragraphs, arranged by what each paragraph is about, to show where the two writers’ concerns overlap and where they diverge.

Ten books in English translation, one star for every paragraph

I. The shelf

Ten books, 5,324 paragraphs

Every star is one paragraph, in reading order: five books by Kafka (2,309 paragraphs) above five by Lispector (3,015). The brighter a star, the closer the other writer comes to it somewhere in their work.

I. The shelf

Every paragraph leaves its shelf

Each star now flies from its place in its book to its place on a map of meaning. The tails trace the journey. On the shelf a paragraph is placed by where it falls in its book; on the map it is placed only by what it says, so paragraphs on similar subjects end up side by side.

II. A first reading

Read for their words, they part into two star clusters

Placed by what a language model makes of their wording, the paragraphs sort themselves by writer. A classifier can tell who wrote an unseen paragraph 93% of the time. What this map shows is two voices, two source languages and a dozen translators.

III. Erasing the voice

Read for their thought, the star clusters break up

Asked for each paragraph’s theme instead of its wording, and with every trace of who wrote it removed, the same stars rearrange. The two writers’ heartlands now interleave. A classifier trained to name the author of an unseen paragraph does little better than a coin toss (55%).

In other words, the direction in the model's space that carried each writer's voice has been removed. A paragraph now sits near paragraphs about similar things, whoever wrote them. A more flexible classifier still finds a weaker trace of the author. For more detail, read the article.

IV. Shared ground

Where neither writer dominates

Each region is coloured by whose paragraphs are denser there, adjusted for Lispector’s larger share of the corpus. Red marks regions where Kafka’s density is at least 1.6 times Lispector’s, and blue marks the reverse.

Green marks regions where the ratio between the two densities falls between 0.64 and 1.6. There, both writers contribute about equally.

V. The sky, named

18 constellations

Each constellation is a region of related thought, named by reading its most central paragraphs. Lines join its brightest stars.

Gregor Samsa’s story, read in order. At each stop the paragraph finds the four Lispector paragraphs nearest to it in thought, and reaches for the nearest.

As the story moves from the bedroom to the family, the thread of the reading wanders across the sky, and so do the places in Lispector that answer it.

VII. Bridges

300 places where each is the other’s nearest

A bridge joins a Kafka paragraph and a Lispector paragraph when each is the other’s closest match. The strongest 300 are drawn.

VIII. A known answer

The two translations

The Metamorphosis is on this map in Susan Bernofsky’s translation. Willa and Edwin Muir translated it too, in 1933, and their version was kept off the map. Both follow the same German paragraphs in the same order, so which paragraph matches which is known without asking any model.

Treat the two translators as if they were the two writers and look for bridges between them. There are 116, and 110 join a paragraph to its own other translation; the other six reach the stretch next door. Asked for its single closest match, a paragraph finds its own translation 90% of the time. Guessing would be right under 1% of the time.

VIII. A known answer

The Muirs’ paragraphs find their twins

Most of Kafka’s Complete Stories on this map is also the Muirs’ work. If the map sorted paragraphs by their translator’s voice, the Muirs’ Metamorphosis would be pulled toward those stories. Searched against all 2,309 Kafka paragraphs, 110 of its 122 paragraphs find their own Bernofsky twin first, and one lands beside another Muir story.

This test is easy. The embeddings from before the voice was erased pass it too (89%), so it does not show that erasing is needed. It shows that erasing does no harm: a paragraph still finds its counterpart in another translator’s words.

VIII. A known answer

How close is a bridge?

Closeness between two paragraphs is scored from about 0, for nothing in common, toward 1, for the same passage. Two Kafka paragraphs picked at random score 0.00; two paragraphs in a row, 0.45; the two ends of a bridge, 0.63; one paragraph in two translations, 0.88.

A bridge sits between the next paragraph of the same story and the same paragraph in another translator’s words. Bridges are chosen because their ends are close, so this scale shows how close they are, not whether they are right.

VIII. A known answer

Swap the translation

56 bridges start in The Metamorphosis, 16 of them among the 300 on the map. Put the Muirs’ words in place of Bernofsky’s, and 21 keep their Lispector partner. Of the other 35, 9 find it second closest and half within the closest five; for a few it falls far down the list.

A bridge found in one translation may not appear in another, so any single bridge is best read as a suggestion.

IX. Sleep

Sleep, in a body that is no longer human

“What if I just go back to sleep for a little while and forget all this foolishness,” he thought, but this proved utterly impossible, for it was his habit to sleep on his right side, and in his present state he was unable to assume this position.Kafka, The Metamorphosis, tr. Susan Bernofsky
I fell asleep as immediately as a roach falls asleep on a vertical wall. There was no human stability in my sleep: it was the balancing power of a roach that falls asleep atop the lime of a wall.Lispector, The Passion According to G.H., tr. Idra Novey

Critics pair these two books for the insect. The map pairs them for something narrower: what sleep becomes once the body has stopped being human.

X. Last songs

Two last works

Josephine’s road, however, must go downhill. The time will soon come when her last notes sound and die into silence.Kafka, “Josephine the Singer, or the Mouse Folk”, tr. Willa and Edwin Muir
Silence. If one day God comes to earth there will be great silence. The silence is such that not even thought thinks.Lispector, The Hour of the Star, tr. Benjamin Moser

“Josephine” is the last story Kafka wrote; The Hour of the Star is the last book Lispector published in her lifetime. Each is about a small, overlooked life, and each closes on silence. The model paired these two paragraphs from their content alone, with authorship erased.

XI. A sound in the night

A sound with no source

But it is this very uniformity of the noise everywhere that disturbs me most, for it cannot be made to agree with my original assumption.Kafka, “The Burrow”, tr. Willa and Edwin Muir
The silence was speaking. It was a soft, constant, flawless treble, all shot through with horizontal and slanting sounds. […] And this is how I was blessed. But I don’t want it ever again.Lispector, “Silent Night, Holy Night”, tr. Margaret Jull Costa and Robin Patterson

Both paragraphs describe a constant sound with no identifiable source. The narrators read it differently: Kafka’s burrower treats it as a threat to be explained, Lispector’s narrator as a blessing she does not want to repeat. The bridge matches the situation, not the response to it.

XII. Alone

Where only one of them goes

Around the rim, each constellation leans toward the writer who fills it. The Stage is 89% Kafka; Being is 72% Lispector; Paradise is 67% Lispector.

Yet even these hold both writers. On the map of their words, whole regions belonged to one of them; once their voices are erased, even the most one-sided constellation, The Stage, holds 5 paragraphs by Lispector among its 44.

XIII. The whole sky

Two writers, one sky

Shown pairs without their source, two language-model readers scored 37% of 35 sampled bridges as the same thought, against 0% of random pairs. Pairs from other methods scored 29% to 46%, so bridges are about as good, not better.

Beside a rival pair, a bridge was preferred 55% of the time, close to a coin toss.

How the map was made, with every number, is in the article.

XIV. A garden of themes

Every constellation holds both writers

Each plant is one constellation, placed in order from the most Kafka (top left) to the most Lispector (bottom right). Each leaf is one paragraph: red for Kafka, blue for Lispector. Its branches are the smaller clusters inside the constellation.

Even the most lopsided plants carry both colours. The green flowers are bridges whose two paragraphs sit in the same constellation, 239 of the 300. Every plant has at least one.

XV. One tree

A tree of 5,324 paragraphs

The trunk holds every paragraph. It splits into three boughs, then seven branches, the broadest groups of related paragraphs. Each of the 18 constellations grows at the end of its own twig.

At every fork, the branch whose constellations lean further toward Kafka grows on the left.
